# Measured tolerances widen some augmentations, gate others

The project's earlier research picked the right augmentations, but it did not settle how strong they can be, and its central caveat, that "no source gives numeric tolerances in millimetres or degrees", no longer holds for location, viewing angle or speed. Motion-capture phonetics now gives location spreads in millimetres. Within one signer, one sign and one condition, the hand's peak position has a token-to-token standard deviation of **11–59 mm**. Faster signing or a low neighbouring sign shifts it **10–43 mm vertically and up to 140 mm sideways, and the sideways rate shifts always point toward the body's midline**. Human-perception studies add recognition thresholds: Deaf signers lose **no significant accuracy between 0° and 45° of yaw**, and isolated-sign identification **breaks down only at 2.5–3× playback speed**. Against these numbers, Tier 1's yaw ±20° and duration 0.8–1.25× are conservative, and the arm-joint edit's 12–14 mm wrist displacement sits inside one standard deviation of a signer's own repetitions. The 2023–2026 machine-learning ablations cut the other way. Single geometric augmentations are worth about 0–1 point on 100-class benchmarks and often vanish in run-to-run noise. Horizontal flip cost 1.3 points on WLASL in one RGB study. Shoulder and wrist rotations of ±7.5° hurt pose-based translation, while elbow rotation of the same size did not. Still missing: degree figures for handshape, palm orientation or movement amplitude; a perceptual location threshold in millimetres; and any per-augmentation ablation on 3-D skeletons at large vocabulary. The net result: two Tier 1 ranges can widen, mirroring becomes a gated augmentation rather than an unconditional one, Tier 2 location shifts get direction-specific magnitudes, and the project inherits a measurement agenda the literature cannot cover.

## The earlier synthesis named the right axes but left them unbounded

The earlier catalogue (Tier 1 view rotation, mirror, uniform rate, small arm-joint rotations, scene scale and uncentring; Tier 2 movement size, location shifts, handshape jitter, weak drop and detector-failure masking) came from two sources. Linguistics said which parameters are contrastive. Recognition practice supplied the ranges people used. That gave a defensible list of what to vary, but each range was borrowed from someone's code rather than from a measured tolerance, and the earlier synthesis said so directly: "Bounds must be set empirically" ([Augmentation_Grounding.md](../../Augmentation_Grounding.md)).

The new evidence corrects four specific points. First, the "no numbers" claim fails for location. Three Optotrak studies publish per-signer means, SDs and shifts in millimetres for forehead and head signs ([Tyrone & Mauk 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2895670/); [Tyrone & Mauk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4489421/); [Mauk & Tyrone 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4606891/)). It also fails for view and speed, where human recognition curves now exist ([Watkins et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/); [Fischer et al. 1999](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/jslhr.4203.568)). Second, the claim that mirroring was "never reported to hurt" is out of date: an RGB study measured **−1.3 points on WLASL with flip** and disabled it there ([Kvanchiani et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11553)). Third, arm-joint rotation now has a measured harm, at ±7.5° for shoulders and wrists in translation ([Železný et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01532)). Fourth, the exclusion of shear is contested: two sign-specific 2-D studies found shear helpful or neutral ([Algafri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16640); [Železný et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01532)).

Other parts of the earlier synthesis still stand. No accessible source reports within-category handshape spreads in degrees, palm-orientation spreads, or movement-amplitude spreads. No published work perturbs MANO hand pose under phonological constraints and measures a recognition gain. The new evidence also has to be read in three distinct kinds, which the sections below keep apart. **Production spreads** say how much real signers vary while producing the same sign. That makes them a realism prior, not a safety limit. **Human-perception thresholds** say how far a stimulus can be pushed before Deaf viewers stop recognising it. That bounds label preservation for humans, not for a classifier. **ML ablations** say whether an augmentation helped a particular model on a particular benchmark. That bounds usefulness, not meaning.

## Signers' own repetitions spread one to six centimetres, and lowering only goes down

The best numeric anchor is location. In Tyrone & Mauk's 2010 study, six native Deaf signers produced the forehead sign WONDER 120 times each. Positions were measured in a head-centred frame with the chin at zero, from a marker on the back of the hand. The within-condition SD of the sign's vertical peak ranged from **10.8 to 59.3 mm** per signer ([Tyrone & Mauk 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2895670/)). A footnote says those SDs pool rate and context effects, so a pure same-condition SD is probably at the low end of that range. The same table shows how far signers differ from each other: mean vertical location for the same sign spans **about 137 mm**, from −23 mm to +114 mm relative to the chin. Much of that gap is likely anatomy and marker placement rather than style. Longer, slower tokens sat higher, with slopes of **+1.6 to +21.6 mm per extra 100 ms of duration** ([Tyrone & Mauk 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2895670/)).

The 2012 follow-up gives the directional structure that augmentation needs ([Tyrone & Mauk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4489421/)). It used four signers and the signs KNOW, WHY, FATHER and STUBBORN. A low neighbouring sign lowered the target by **10–40 mm in 7 of 14 comparisons**, shifted it laterally by 15–88 mm, and moved it front–back by only 5–13 mm. Rate effects were larger: up to 42.9 mm vertically, **139.7 mm laterally** and 118.8 mm front–back. Some signers rose rather than fell with rate. The lateral rate shifts were one-sided, and the authors put it plainly: "No significant rightward shifts occurred as an effect of rate". For right-handers that means toward the midline. The authors also argue that forward or sideways shifts "cannot create a different lexical item" ([Tyrone & Mauk 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4489421/)). For one signer, the lateral extent of signing space was **275 mm at normal rate and 285 mm at fast rate**, about a 4% difference. At fast rate the low-left endpoint dropped by about 150 mm, and the height of KNOW tracked the following sign (r² = 0.78) far more than the preceding one (r² = 0.14) ([Mauk & Tyrone 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4606891/)).

A conversational video corpus shows the same asymmetry at scale: 3,075 tokens from six signers, measured in pixels from each sign's canonical location ([Russell, Wilkinson & Janzen 2011](https://home.cc.umanitoba.ca/~krussll/papers/Russell,%20Wilkinson,%20and%20Janzen%202011%20draft.pdf)). Forehead signs such as BROTHER, FATHER and MAN have long downward tails, while their chin or mouth partners SISTER, MOTHER and WOMAN stay tightly clustered. The eye is a forbidden zone about 30 px below the forehead target. Verbs are lowered about 7.9 px more than nouns, and each step up in subjective frequency adds 4.5 px of lowering. Converting to millimetres needs an assumed face scale, which the paper does not give. If the forehead-to-eye distance is taken as about 3–4 cm, the noun mean is roughly half a centimetre. The clearer message is qualitative: **tolerance is direction- and region-dependent**. It is large and downward at the forehead, small at the chin and mouth, and bounded by the eye.

Timing varies far more than the 0.8–1.25× range assumes. For the same sign and context, WONDER averaged **603 ms for one signer and 1,086 ms for another, about 1.8×**, with within-signer coefficients of variation of roughly 25–55% ([Tyrone & Mauk 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2895670/)). ASL-LEX citation signs from a single model signer average 851 ± 301 ms ([Sehyr et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7977685)). Speed and space are coupled, though. Faster tokens are lower and more central, so a pure temporal speed-up with no spatial change is somewhat less realistic at the fast end.

For the hand itself, the evidence stays qualitative. Glove studies report calibration accuracy and correlations, not within-category SDs. The clearest example is fingerspelled E: one signer's index knuckle (MCP) was slightly flexed and another's slightly hyperextended. A single letter category therefore spans both sides of 0° at that joint, and the authors say the amount of acceptable phonetic variation between handshapes is unknown ([Eccarius, Bour & Scheidt 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3758436/)). Coarticulation studies agree on which fingers are free. In 728 NGT tokens of B-handshape signs from 61 signers, the thumb state and finger spread of neighbouring signs predicted the target's thumb and spread ([de Meijer et al. 2017](https://doaj.org/article/68451f72ec534227bd6f584f15fb7006)). In ASL fingerspelling, the pinky shows less coarticulatory extension when it is a selected, flexed finger than when it is non-selected ([Keane, Brentari & Riggle 2015](https://voices.uchicago.edu/lvc/?p=427)). The pattern is that **non-selected fingers and the thumb in open handshapes vary freely, while selected fingers resist**, but no source puts degrees on it. Palm orientation has only error rates. Fluent signers reversed in–out orientation in 0.23% of imitations and up–down orientation in 1.16%, and misproduced lateral movement direction in 6.3% ([Shield & Meier 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00811/full)). Orientation and direction flips are therefore not natural variation. The large ASL mocap corpus that could answer these questions, CUNY with 8 signers in data gloves, publishes no per-sign variance statistics ([Lu & Huenerfauth 2013](https://huenerfauth.ist.rit.edu/pubs/lu-huenerfauth-2013-csl-preprint.pdf)).

| Production spread (natural variation, not a threshold) | Value | Source and sample |
|---|---|---|

These are spreads from four to six signers producing head-area signs in a laboratory. They describe what real production does. They do not prove that an arbitrary shift of the same size keeps the sign, and they say nothing directly about neutral-space signs.

## Deaf viewers tolerate 45° of yaw and fast playback, but handshape is where they slip

For viewpoint, the most transferable evidence is a 2024 BSL study. Five cameras on a 180° arc filmed one signer **simultaneously**, which is exactly equivalent to rotating a 3-D skeleton or camera ([Watkins et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)). Forty-five signers made semantic decisions on 240 noun signs. Accuracy was **95.3% at 0°, 92.8% at 45° and 89.8% at 90°**, and 0° and 45° did not differ significantly. Reaction time did rise at every step, from 1,884 to 1,952 to 2,046 ms. Less fluent signers lost disproportionately more accuracy at non-frontal angles. Mental-rotation ability correlated with L1 Deaf signers' reaction times at r = 0.734, which fits a frontal canonical representation that viewers mentally rotate. An ASL priming study also found viewpoint-specific priming in Deaf signers as well as non-signers ([Corina, Grosvald & Lachaud 2011](https://vl2.gallaudet.edu/center-papers/294)). The arc was horizontal only, so there is **no human evidence for pitch or roll**.

For speed, 14 native Deaf ASL signers watched isolated signs and sentences at 1–6× normal playback. Isolated-sign identification fell from about 95% to 46%, processing broke down at about **2.5–3×**, and at the breakdown point errors shifted from semantic to formational ([Fischer, Delhorne & Reed 1999](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/jslhr.4203.568)). This is read from the abstract; the per-rate values at 2× and 3× were not obtained. No human study of slowed signing was found. Avatar research adds a structural point. Signers preferred faster signs, slower transitions and human-like pauses ([Al-Khazraji et al. 2021](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/at-a-different-pace-evaluating-whether-users-prefer-timing-parame/)). Comprehension improved with linguistically placed pauses, and the gain came from where the pauses went, not from extra time ([Huenerfauth 2009](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1530064.1530067)). Uniform scaling keeps that hold-and-transition structure. Non-uniform warps do not.

For handedness, native signers showed "little evidence for handedness congruency effects" in lexical decision on mirror-reversed video, while late learners did show such effects ([Corina & Gutierrez 2016](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/gest.15.3.01cor); described in [Brozdowski & Emmorey 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7387226)). In BSL, 43 Deaf signers, including left-handers, matched one-handed and symmetrical signs faster when the model was right-handed. The authors attribute this to exposure frequency rather than recognisability ([Watkins & Thompson 2017](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news-archive/2017/right-or-left-handedness-affects-sign-language-comprehension)). A whole-body mirror is therefore a perceptually natural variant. No perception study has tested signs whose lateral side carries meaning.

Degradation studies show which information carries identity. With point-light displays, **only fingertip motion was necessary** for identification ([Poizner, Bellugi & Lutes-Driscoll 1981](https://scilit.com/publications/516cdb43d15a38c0a62ebf7b02c08d95)). Fluent users communicated easily from 27 light spots on the hands and face ([Tartter & Knowlton 1981](https://www.nature.com/articles/289676a0.pdf)). Perceived intelligibility stopped improving above **10 fps** ([Tran et al. 2015](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/evaluating-intelligibility-and-battery-drain-of-mobile-sign-langu/)), and frames chosen at event boundaries beat every-m-th-frame sampling ([Parish, Sperling & Landy 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2142199)). When Deaf BSL signers misperceived signs, they changed **movement and handshape more often than location** ([Orfanidou et al. 2009](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/MC.37.3.302)). Categorical-perception work points the same way. An LSF study with 11-step avatar continua found categorical perception for a phonemic handshape pair only, none for an allophonic handshape pair, and none for place of articulation. ASL replications for handshape are mixed ([GLOW 45 abstract 106](https://glowlinguistics.org/45/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2022/04/GLOW_45_abstract_106.pdf)). None of these sources reports physical step sizes. No avatar study reports a threshold for amplitude, location offset in centimetres, or joint-angle error. One practical constraint from that literature is that robotic, procedurally animated motion is rejected ([Naert, Larboulette & Gibet 2020](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03005767)). That supports smoothing and joint-limit constraints on any perturbation.

| Human-perception evidence (recognition by people) | Threshold or result | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Yaw of true multi-camera views (BSL, 45 signers) | 0° vs 45°: no accuracy difference; 90°: −5.5 points; reaction time slower at every step | Yaw up to ±45° keeps the label for humans |
| Playback speed (ASL, 14 native signers) | breakdown at 2.5–3×; isolated signs 95% → 46% over 1–6× | 1.5× faster is well inside the safe range |
| Mirror-reversed video (ASL; BSL) | no congruency cost for native signers; a frequency-driven reaction-time edge for right-handed models | Whole-body mirror is natural; side-meaningful signs untested |
| Point-light and frame rate (ASL) | fingertip motion necessary; ceiling at 10 fps | Preserve fingertip paths; frame drop down to about 10 fps is safe |
| Misperception by parameter (BSL) | movement and handshape errors more common than location errors | Hand and movement perturbations need the tightest bounds |
| Categorical perception (LSF, ASL) | categorical for phonemic handshape only; never for location | Handshape has a hard boundary; location is gradient but has minimal pairs |

## Recent ablations find small gains, real noise and two specific harms

The 2023–2026 ML evidence is thinner than the perception evidence and comes from a different setting. **No paper ablates augmentations one at a time on 3-D skeletons at large vocabulary.** The per-augmentation sweeps are on 100-class or 20-class 2-D keypoint tasks, on RGB, or on translation. Within that, the clearest result is a 2-D OpenPose sweep on a corrected 100-class WLASL, run several times. In-plane rotation from 1° to 32° stayed within about ±0.56 points of a 79.26% baseline, the best being +0.43 at 8°, and the authors call this "no measurable effect". Scale up to ±0.32 per axis was also within noise. Large keypoint noise (λ = 0.512) cost 3.4 points, and trimming 32 frames from the start or offset-padding by 32 frames cost 1.8 and 2.1 points ([Woods & Rana 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672608)). Another 2-D WLASL-100 study measured ±13° rotation alone at +0.46, Gaussian noise alone at +0.07, and both together at +1.09. Its keypoint-reconstruction preprocessing alone gave **+6.05**, and it reports that MediaPipe misses almost 50% of hands in WLASL ([Roh et al. 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.signlang-1.36.pdf)). A 20-class semi-supervised study found signing-space normalisation worth +12.3 points on test, against +4.6 for adding shear on top of rotation, and −1.6 for noise alone ([Algafri et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.16640)). Across these three papers, **normalisation and pose cleaning outweigh any single geometric augmentation by roughly 3–10×**.

Two measured harms bear directly on Tier 1. In pose-based translation trained on YouTube-ASL and evaluated zero-shot on How2Sign, ±4.5° in-plane rotation was the worst augmentation (BLEU-1 17.47 → 15.30). **Shoulder rotation of ±7.5° (16.39) and wrist rotation of ±7.5° (16.05) also hurt, while elbow rotation of ±7.5° was neutral (17.48).** The authors argue that shoulder and wrist rotation "can easily change the meaning of signs", and their final protocol keeps only shear, elbow rotation and noise ([Železný et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.01532)). On mirroring, an RGB isolated-sign study found flip lowered WLASL accuracy by 1.3 points and raised AUTSL by 0.11. On Slovo it applied flip at p = 0.5 but blacklisted four side-dependent signs, including "left", "right", "heart" and "liver" ([Kvanchiani et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11553)). The pose evidence runs the other way: removing flip cost 0.69 points on WLASL100 ([Laines et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.05403)), and normalising handedness in fingerspelling changed nothing ([Shi 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12419)). The NGT200 authors suggest flip augmentation but warn that "some signs encode directionality" ([Ranum et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15284)). Flip's net effect is therefore small, about ±1 point, and depends on the dataset and model. No per-class analysis of where it hurts has been published.

On view and time, the ML evidence is indirect. No study sweeps 3-D yaw magnitude for sign recognition. The only verified gain from SMPL-X view rotation remains Spoken2Sign's ±20° (+1.46 WLASL, +1.29 MSASL) ([Zuo et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.04730)). NGT200 shows why view coverage can matter: 2-D pose models trained on a single camera lost **more than 50% relative accuracy on ±25° views**, and multi-view training recovered much of that ([Ranum et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15284)). In RGB isolated-sign recognition, both ×2 speed-up and ×2 slow-down at p = 0.25 helped a little: removing them cost 0.32 and 0.66 points on SlovoExt ([Kvanchiani et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.11553)). In skeleton pre-training for translation, a 0.5 duration scale hurt (BLEU-4 12.27 against 13.68 unscaled) while 1.5 helped (14.71) ([Walsh, Ivashechkin & Bowden 2025](https://personalpages.surrey.ac.uk/r.bowden/publications/2025/Walsh_SLTAT2025pp.pdf)). The strongest recent large-vocabulary skeleton models either use flip, noise and crop without ablating them ([Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.12519)) or use no input augmentation at all ([Pu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.29057)).

Hand-level precedents remain sparse, and none of them gives a usable tolerance. The 2nd-place GISLR solution rotates distal finger sub-chains about proximal joints ("finger-tree rotate"), with an unspecified angle and probabilities tuned in about 0.1–0.5. It reports no ablation ([ffs333 GISLR 2nd place](https://github.com/ffs333/2nd_place_GISLR)). The 1st-place fingerspelling solution applies a global affine (scale 0.8–1.2, shear ±0.15, shift ±0.1, rotation ±30°, p = 0.75) and resamples speed at 0.5–1.5 with p = 0.8 ([Henkel et al. cfg_1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ChristofHenkel/kaggle-asl-fingerspelling-1st-place-solution/main/configs/cfg_1.py)). Its library defines FingersDrop and HandDrop transforms, but that config does not use them ([Henkel et al. augmentations](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ChristofHenkel/kaggle-asl-fingerspelling-1st-place-solution/main/configs/augmentations.py)). Its ±30° is a whole-skeleton rotation and says nothing about finger tolerance. In an Indonesian (BISINDO) alphabet study, four anatomical 3-D hand-keypoint augmentations raised cross-domain F1 from **67.20% to 89.55%**, but the training data came from a single subject and the accessible text gives neither ranges nor a per-transform ablation ([Alfarisi et al. 2026](https://journal.unnes.ac.id/journals/sji/article/view/42221)). Whole-hand rotation or cropping on hand-gesture benchmarks is worth about +0.5 to +1.3 points ([AugmentGest 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.07216v1)).

Synthetic data gives a scheduling lesson more than a range. On NGT200, adding an avatar driven by motion-capture animations, rendered at three views, raised unseen-signer front-view accuracy from **0.14 to 0.43**, roughly as much as an extra human signer, on a dataset of only two training signers ([Ranum et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.15284)). In skeleton translation, stitched synthetic data **helped as pre-training (BLEU-4 11.32 → 13.68) but slightly hurt when mixed with real data (10.73)** ([Walsh, Ivashechkin & Bowden 2025](https://personalpages.surrey.ac.uk/r.bowden/publications/2025/Walsh_SLTAT2025pp.pdf)). For handshapes, GAN pretraining raised RWTH-PHOENIX handshape accuracy from 80.62% to 85.34%. A pose-conditioned generator trained only on the dataset's own poses barely moved it (80.91%), and generated data did not help on the large HaGRID set ([Rios et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.17008v1)). **New pose diversity, not re-rendering of poses already present, is what paid off, and it paid off most on rare classes.**

For an invariance check, the Sem-Lex phonology models are the most usable ready-made tool. An SL-GCN with one head per ASL-LEX feature reaches 91.1% on selected fingers, 81.2% on flexion, 88.4% on spread, 91.5% on thumb position and 77.4% on the 58-class handshape ([Kezar et al. 2023, Sem-Lex](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.00196)), and code is public ([Kezar et al. 2023, phonology](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.00195)). Those accuracies are the check's noise floor. A classifier that is wrong 19% of the time on flexion cannot detect a jitter-induced flexion flip below that rate, so flexion needs an independent geometric check.

## Catalogue verdicts: two ranges widen, mirror gains a gate, location gets millimetres

### Tier 1 changes

Only two Tier 1 ranges have grounds to change. **Duration** can widen because every kind of evidence agrees that 0.8–1.25× is narrow. Natural between-signer spread is about 1.8×, human identification holds well past 1.5× faster, and RGB ablations found ×2 in both directions helpful. The one measured harm, a 0.5 duration scale applied to every pre-training sequence, marks the outer edge, and a range of about 0.67–1.5× stays inside it. **View yaw** could widen to ±45° on human evidence, but the ML case is weaker. On frontal test data, rotation-type augmentations gain little and can hurt translation, so ±45° belongs in an ablation, not in the default.

**Mirror** is the one Tier 1 item whose status drops. Mirroring stays perceptually natural for lexical signs, but a measured −1.3-point harm on WLASL, together with an existing blacklist precedent, means it should carry a per-sign gate and a tuned probability. The arm-joint range does not change. The new harm appears at almost twice the current magnitude and only at the shoulder and wrist, and the current 12–14 mm wrist displacement is now known to be smaller than a signer's own repetition spread.

### Tier 2 magnitudes and directions

**Location shifts** can now be specified in millimetres. These numbers are production spreads, used as a realism prior, and are not perceptual limits. They should be applied in a face- or torso-anchored frame. For body-anchored signs, a per-clip offset with **σ ≈ 10 mm, truncated at about 20 mm**, stays at the low end of the measured token-to-token SD (10.8 mm and up). That low end is the right reference, because the larger SDs pool rate and context. On top of that offset, a **lateral shift toward the midline of up to about 40–50 mm** matches the coarticulation shifts (15–88 mm) and rate shifts reported for right-handers. The shift should never point away from the midline, since no significant outward shifts were observed. **Forehead signs** may be lowered **downward only, by about 10–40 mm**, which is the attested range for environment and rate effects. Lowering must stop short of the eye and is blocked when a lower-location minimal pair exists (BROTHER/SISTER, FATHER/MOTHER). **Chin, mouth and nose signs** get no lowering and tighter jitter. Their tight video clusters justify a smaller bound, though no source gives one in millimetres. **Neutral-space signs** were not measured in these studies, so the head-sign numbers are a floor rather than a ceiling for them.

**Handshape jitter** still has no degree bounds, but the evidence now says how to shape it. The jitter should be larger on non-selected fingers and the thumb of open handshapes, smaller on selected fingers, and smallest along the flexion axis, the feature the phonology classifier also resolves worst. It should preserve fingertip paths, which carry identity for human viewers. Rare handshape classes should get priority, since that is where generative handshape data paid off. One inference from the fingerspelled-E observation is a span of at least ±10–20° at some MCP joints, but that comes from qualitative text, not a measured SD, and should be treated as a hypothesis to test.

### Excluded list

The new evidence reinforces most exclusions and contests one. **Temporal crops** now have direct pose-ISLR harm (−1.8 and −2.1 points for 32-frame trims and offsets) ([Woods & Rana 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10672608)). **Heavy joint noise** hurts in two studies. **Orientation and movement-direction flips** are not natural variation: fluent signers misproduce them in 0.2–1.2% and 6.3% of imitations. **Non-uniform warps** gain indirect support for their exclusion, because avatar studies show that pause and transition placement drives comprehension, though no study shows such warps change a sign's identity. **Camera roll** should be added to the excluded list. It harmed translation at ±4.5°, has no human evidence behind it, and adds nothing that yaw and pitch do not already cover.

**Shear** is the contested case. A 20-class study found +4.6 points from shear, SPOTER-style squeeze is used widely, and the translation ablation kept shear. All of that is 2-D image-space shear, which roughly imitates perspective. In a 3-D SMPL-X skeleton, shear distorts bone lengths and joint geometry, so it should stay excluded there. If the downstream model consumes projected 2-D keypoints, a small output-space shear or perspective term is a legitimate item to ablate. A better long-term substitute is a real camera model: view rotation followed by perspective projection.

## What the literature cannot tell the project

Five gaps remain open after this pass, and no amount of further reading is likely to close them. First, **no study gives a perceptual location threshold in millimetres**, meaning the displacement at which signers start to re-identify a sign. The millimetre figures above are production spreads, and categorical-perception work found no place-of-articulation boundary to measure. Second, **no source gives within-category handshape spreads in degrees, palm-orientation spreads, or movement-amplitude spreads**. The likely sources (Keane 2014, Cheek 2001, Crasborn 2001, the Emmorey et al. 2009 kinematics paper, the CUNY corpus) were unreachable or publish no variance tables. Third, **slowed signing, pitch and roll** have no human-perception evidence. Fourth, **no per-class analysis of mirror harm** exists, so the blacklist has to be built from linguistic judgment and the project's own per-class deltas. Fifth, **no per-augmentation ablation exists for 3-D skeletons at large vocabulary**. The newer sweeps also show that single augmentations sit inside run-to-run noise at 100 classes, so any ablation that does not use several seeds will mislead.

Four measurements follow, and none can be borrowed from the literature. The first is the natural spread in the project's own data. Using the reconstructed SMPL-X and MANO clips of the same gloss across WLASL signers, the project should compute location SDs per ASL-LEX minor location in a face-anchored frame, the forehead lowering distribution, MANO joint-angle spreads per handshape category (separately for selected and non-selected fingers), palm-normal spreads per orientation class, and path-length spreads per gloss. The location figures should be compared with the 11–59 mm Optotrak range. The second is the pipeline's noise floor in the same frame, from the round-trip hand-to-face error. A 10 mm jitter is meaningless if the reconstruction already moves the hand by that much. The third is calibration of the invariance checks. The phonology classifier's flip rate on unaugmented re-fits sets the baseline that augmented clips must not exceed, and flexion needs a geometric check because the classifier gets flexion wrong about 19% of the time. The fourth is a one-at-a-time classifier ablation on a signer-independent, large-vocabulary split such as ASL Citizen or WLASL2000, with at least three to five seeds. Its arms should include yaw ±20° against ±45°, duration 0.8–1.25× against 0.67–1.5×, mirror p = 0 against 0.5 with per-class deltas to populate the blacklist, the elbow-weighted arm rotation, and pre-training against mixing. Only that last measurement can show whether any augmentation helps, as distinct from whether it keeps the sign.

## Conclusion

The new evidence splits the catalogue along a line the earlier synthesis did not draw. For Tier 1, the binding constraint is not meaning. Human viewers tolerate far more yaw and speed change than the current ranges apply, and four of the six Tier 1 transforms (view, scale, uncentre, duration) are rigid or whole-scene, so they cannot move a phonological parameter by construction. The binding constraint there is usefulness: recent ablations show these augmentations buy about a point at most, often less than normalisation and pose cleaning. For Tier 2, the binding constraint is meaning, and the new numbers make it concrete. Location tolerance is a direction-dependent region of a few centimetres, not a radius: generous downward at the forehead, generous toward the midline, tight at the chin, and zero across a minimal pair. Handshape tolerance is anisotropic by finger role, and no one has yet measured it in degrees.

The most consequential implication is about where gains will come from. The handshape GAN result and the NGT200 avatar result both say that recognisers improve when they see variation the dataset lacks, not when they see their own poses re-rendered. If that holds, KineticAugment's value lies in the Tier 2 edits, which create plausible variation that real signers produce but small datasets under-sample: location displacement, rate-coupled lowering and within-category handshape spread. It does not lie in widening Tier 1 further. That makes the project's own measurements of natural spread the next step, not an optional calibration, because they are the only source of Tier 2 magnitudes that are both realistic and specific to this pipeline.
