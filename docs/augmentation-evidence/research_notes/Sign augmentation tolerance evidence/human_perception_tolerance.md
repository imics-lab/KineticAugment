# Human perception tolerance: how far can a sign be transformed and still be recognised?

Scope: perception and intelligibility studies with human (mostly Deaf) viewers, covering mirroring and handedness, viewpoint, speed, degraded input, signing avatars, and categorical-perception continua. The aim is to bound geometry-aware augmentation of 3-D skeletons for isolated signs (ASL, WLASL-style).

Evidence labels:
- **VERIFIED**: I read the primary full text or the publisher or index abstract myself.
- **VERIFIED-SECONDARY**: the claim comes from a later paper that describes the work, or from the abstract text a search index returned when the publisher page itself was blocked (403).
- **EXISTS**: the paper is confirmed to exist, but its content was not checked.
- **UNVERIFIED**: the claim comes from a tertiary summary or review site.

The sign language is given for every claim.

## 1. Mirroring and handedness: do Deaf signers recognise left-handed (mirrored) signs as well as right-handed ones?

### Takeaway
Native signers show little or no recognition cost for mirror-reversed (left-dominant) signs in lexical decision. Late and non-native learners do show a handedness-congruency cost. In a separate BSL study, all viewers, including left-handers, were faster for right-handed models on one-handed and symmetrical signs. That advantage is attributed to how often viewers see right-handed signing. No accuracy drop was reported. I found no millisecond-level effect sizes in accessible text.

### Cited Findings
- **ASL, mirror-reversed video.** Corina & Gutierrez (2016), *Gesture* 15(3), 291–305, doi:10.1075/gest.15.3.01cor. In a lexical-decision task, signs either matched or mismatched the observer's dominant hand. Native signers showed "little evidence for handedness congruency effects". Non-native late learners and hearing ASL–English bilinguals did show congruency effects. The authors interpret this as native representations being less tied to the producing hand (a forward-model account). Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract text via index pages). — [jbe-platform](https://www.jbe-platform.com/content/journals/10.1075/gest.15.3.01cor); [CNLSE record](https://cnlse.es/es/recursos/biblioteca/embodiment-and-american-sign-language-exploring-sensory-motor-influences-recognition)
- **The left-handed stimuli were mirror-reversed videos.** Brozdowski & Emmorey (2020) describe Corina & Gutierrez (2016) as testing right-handed signers on "right-handed or mirror-reversed left-handed signed stimuli". They also report that faster responses for handedness-aligned items occurred only for non-native signers. Label: VERIFIED (full text of Brozdowski & Emmorey 2020, *Acta Psychologica* 208, 103092, read via PMC). — [PMC7387226](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7387226)
- **BSL, 43 Deaf fluent signers, picture–sign matching.** Watkins & Thompson (2017), *Cognition* 164, 144–149. Right- and left-handed participants were both faster with right-handed models for one-handed and two-handed symmetrical signs. For two-handed asymmetrical signs, left-handers were faster with left-handed models. The authors attribute the right-model advantage to frequency of exposure and propose a weak motor-theory account. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY for the N and the pattern (university press release and Brozdowski & Emmorey 2020). The abstract is VERIFIED in our existing `linguistics.md` 3.3. — [Birmingham news](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/news-archive/2017/right-or-left-handedness-affects-sign-language-comprehension); [PMC7387226](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7387226)
- **Viewing your own signing from the signer's perspective is hard.** Emmorey, Bosworth & Kraljic (2009), *J. Memory and Language* 61, 398–411 (ASL). Signers were poor at recognising signs shown as they would look during self-production. Note that this manipulation is a perspective change (viewed from behind or above), not a left–right mirror. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract content via search index; no accuracy numbers obtained). — [PMC2637109 / PubMed 20161058](https://www.pubmed.gov/20161058/)
- **Signers learn to reverse rather than mirror when imitating.** Shield & Meier (2018), *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:811, a review. Children must learn that signs are specified for the dominant and non-dominant hand, not for right or left. Adult sign exposure shifts imitation from mirroring to reversal. Label: VERIFIED (abstract). — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.00811)

### Inferences
- A left–right mirrored skeleton (dominant hand swapped, lateral movement directions reversed) is a natural, attested variant. Native Deaf viewers recognise it with no measurable lexical-decision cost (Corina & Gutierrez) and, at most, a frequency-driven reaction-time disadvantage (Watkins & Thompson). This is the strongest human-perception support for horizontal-flip augmentation, as long as the flip is applied to the whole body (both hands and any lateral path). Flipping only one hand would not be a real left-handed signer.
- The mirrored variant is still lower-frequency input. Recognition models may face a similar distribution shift, so mirroring at 50% may over-represent left-handed signing compared with real data. Real data has roughly 10% left-handed signers, but I found no sign-specific estimate.
- Exception that remains: signs whose lateral location is lexically meaningful. Examples are indexing a fixed referent and some directional or agreement verbs. These would not be meaning-preserving under mirroring. This is a linguistic argument; I found no perception study that measured it.

### Gaps
- I could not get exact reaction-time differences in milliseconds or accuracy values for either Corina & Gutierrez (2016) or Watkins & Thompson (2017). Their full texts were blocked (403) or could not be extracted as PDFs. The size of the right-model advantage is therefore unknown.
- I could not confirm whether Watkins & Thompson used real left-handed models or flipped video.
- I found no study that mirrored signs with a lexically significant lateral direction.

## 2. Viewpoint: recognition accuracy as a function of viewing angle

### Takeaway
BSL signers viewing camera-captured signs lost **no significant accuracy between 0° and 45° yaw**: 95.3% versus 92.8%. At **90° (side view)** accuracy fell to 89.8% and responses were about **160 ms slower**. Every pairwise reaction-time step was significant, so 45° already costs about 70 ms. Less fluent signers are hurt more. Signers appear to store a frontal representation and mentally rotate other views.

### Cited Findings
- **Design (BSL).** Watkins et al. (2024), "Viewing angle matters in British Sign Language processing", *Scientific Reports*. There were 45 right-handed BSL signers: 12 L1 Deaf, 9 L1 hearing, 11 advanced L2, and 13 intermediate L2. The task was a semantic decision ("is it living?" or "is it edible?") within a distance-priming design of 480 trials. There were 240 noun signs, each filmed **simultaneously** by 5 cameras on a 180° arc at 0°, 45°L, 45°R, 90°L and 90°R, from 1.5 m away at 1.2 m height. A single right-handed Deaf L1 model signed, and videos were 382×400 px. Label: VERIFIED (full text, PMC). — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)
- **Accuracy by angle (all groups pooled):** 0° 95.3% (SD 4.1), 45° 92.8% (SD 4.99), 90° 89.8% (SD 5.92). The main effect of angle was χ²=17.20, p<0.001. 90° was less accurate than 0° (Z=4.53) and than 45° (Z=4.2). **0° and 45° did not differ.** Label: VERIFIED. — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)
- **Reaction time by angle:** 0° 1884 ms, 45° 1952 ms, 90° 2046 ms (χ²=14.92, p<0.001). All pairwise differences were significant. Label: VERIFIED. — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)
- **By group (angles pooled):** L1 Deaf 95.6% at 1701 ms; L1 hearing 93.9%; advanced L2 92.7%; intermediate L2 88.3% at 2190 ms. The angle × group interaction was not significant for accuracy (p=0.21) or reaction time (p=0.24). The angle × BSL fluency score interaction for accuracy was significant (p=0.014): less fluent signers were disproportionately less accurate at non-frontal angles. Label: VERIFIED. — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)
- **Sign type:** one-handed signs averaged 1948 ms and two-handed asymmetrical signs 2081 ms. Thirteen of the 240 items (5.4%) were excluded for accuracy below 70%. Label: VERIFIED. — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)
- **Mental rotation:** for L1 Deaf signers, mental-rotation ability correlated with BSL reaction time at r=0.734. The authors propose a frontal canonical representation that signers mentally rotate. Label: VERIFIED. — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)
- **Viewpoint-specific priming (ASL).** Corina, Grosvald & Lachaud (2011), *Language and Cognitive Processes* 26(8), 1102–1135. Repetition priming was larger for identical prime–target views than for views that differed, in both Deaf signers and hearing non-signers. Viewpoint sensitivity was therefore not reduced by sign experience. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via index snippets). — [Gallaudet VL2 record](https://vl2.gallaudet.edu/center-papers/294)
- **Other viewpoint work.** Watkins et al. (2024) cite several related studies: Brozdowski et al. (ASL classifiers; 90° side views were faster than face-on for classifier constructions placed in the interlocutor's signing space), Quinto-Pozos et al. (a 45° over-the-shoulder view with children), and Peterson (a reported viewer preference for about a 30° offset). Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (cited in the full text of Watkins et al. 2024). — [PMC10781993](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10781993/)

### Inferences
- For isolated lexical signs, a global yaw rotation of the 3-D skeleton up to **±45°** sits inside the range where human accuracy is statistically unchanged, about a 2.5-point drop. That range is well supported. Up to **±90°**, signs remain highly recognisable (about 90%) but processing is measurably harder. For a recogniser, ±90° is a stronger augmentation than humans "need", but the signs are still label-preserving.
- These stimuli are true multi-camera views of one physical production, which is exactly equivalent to rotating the 3-D skeleton or camera. The numbers transfer to skeleton yaw augmentation better than most avatar studies do.
- Viewpoints were tested only on a horizontal arc (yaw). There is no human evidence here for pitch or roll changes.

### Gaps
- Per-angle accuracy broken down by group is reported only in figures (Figures 3 and 4), not as numbers in the text.
- No left-versus-right 45° asymmetry figures were extracted. The full text pools 45°L and 45°R.
- I found no study of avatar viewing angle with Deaf participants that reports accuracy by angle.

## 3. Speed: time-compressed and time-expanded sign

### Takeaway
ASL intelligibility **breaks down at about 2.5–3× normal playback rate**. With 14 native Deaf signers, isolated-sign identification fell from about 95% (1×) to 46%, and key-sign identification in sentences fell from 88% to 19%, over a 1–6× range. At the breakdown point, errors shift from semantic to formational. No human study of slowed-down (expanded) sign was found. Avatar work shows Deaf viewers *prefer* sign durations about 1.6× shorter than human values, while keeping human-like pauses.

### Cited Findings
- **ASL.** Fischer, Delhorne & Reed (1999), "Effects of rate of presentation on the reception of American Sign Language", *J. Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* 42(3), 568–582, doi:10.1044/jslhr.4203.568. Fourteen native Deaf ASL signers watched isolated signs and sentences at **1×, 2×, 3×, 4× and 6×** normal playback speed. Isolated-sign identification fell from about **95% to 46%**. Identification of key signs in sentences fell from **88% to 19%**. Processing broke down at about **2.5–3×**, with a shift of errors from semantic to formational. The authors posit a modality-independent upper limit on language-processing rate. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract text returned by the search index; the ASHA and ScienceGate pages returned 403). — [ASHA](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/abs/10.1044/jslhr.4203.568); [ScienceGate](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1044/jslhr.4203.568)
- **Tactile ASL (deaf-blind receivers).** Reed et al. (1995), *JSHR* 38, 477–489. Sentence performance was relatively independent of rate over about 1–3 signs per second. This is a different modality from video, so it is only a comparison. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via search snippet). — [ScienceGate](https://next.sciencegate.app/document/10.1044/jshr.3802.477)
- **ASL avatar timing preferences.** Al-Khazraji, Dingman, Lee & Huenerfauth (2021), "At a Different Pace…", ASSETS 2021, doi:10.1145/3441852.3471214. Two studies with 20 ASL signers each varied sign duration, transition time, differential signing rate, pause length and pause frequency. Signers preferred pauses close to human values. They preferred **faster signs, slower transitions** and less differential-rate variation than humans produce. Label: VERIFIED (abstract on the NJIT research portal). — [NJIT](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/at-a-different-pace-evaluating-whether-users-prefer-timing-parame/)
- **Preferred duration value.** The preferred sign duration was about **0.81 s, against a human-typical 1.28 s** (ratio about 0.63, or about 1.6× faster). Label: UNVERIFIED (third-party review site; not in the abstract I read). — [a11y-paradise summary](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69e25c58a15cc4758c45daf6)
- **ASL avatar timing and comprehension.** Huenerfauth (2008, ASSETS; 2009, *ACM TACCESS*, doi:10.1145/1530064.1530067). Native signers watched 12 passages at three speeds, with and without linguistically motivated pause and timing insertion. The timing model significantly raised comprehension scores overall and at "normal" speed. Animations with pauses that were sped back up to the original duration still outperformed unpaused ones, so the benefit came from where pauses were placed, not from added time. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY. — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1530064.1530067); [RUCCS talk](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/talks/event-details/1755-a-linguistic-timing-model-for-)
- **Pause benefit size.** Comprehension questions were answered "about 45% more often" with pause-processed animations. Label: UNVERIFIED (review site). — [a11y-paradise](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69cdd6ae6885b2590f080e75)

### Inferences
- Uniform time compression up to **about 2×** has not been shown to cause the breakdown, which begins around 2.5–3×. **About 1.5×** is very likely safe; avatar users even prefer it for the sign-internal portion. For isolated signs, a conservative label-preserving range of about 0.67–1.5× in duration (playback rate 1.5× faster) fits this evidence. Going beyond 2× faster is not supported.
- Fischer et al. compressed whole video, including transitions and holds. Compressing only within a sign while keeping holds and pauses is closer to what avatar users prefer.
- **Time expansion (slow motion) is not covered** by any human study found. Intuitively it is less risky for identification, because no information is lost. However, slowed movement could alter the perceived contrast between movement and hold, or between a single and repeated movement, and this is untested.

### Gaps
- The abstract does not give the exact per-rate intelligibility values at 2× and 3×. I also could not confirm whether 46% and 19% were measured at the maximum rate of 6×. The full text was blocked (403).
- I could not get the speed values for the three conditions in Huenerfauth (2008).
- I found no study of time-expanded (slowed) sign intelligibility, and no study of non-uniform time warping such as speeding one phase.

## 4. Reduced or degraded input: point-light, frame rate, resolution, bandwidth

### Takeaway
Sign identity survives very heavy degradation. Point-light displays of 27 spots on the hands and face support fluent ASL communication, and **fingertip motion is the one essential point set**. About **10 fps** marks the intelligibility ceiling: going above it gives no gain in perceived intelligibility. Binary or cartoon images at a few kbit/s keep about 86% of full-bandwidth intelligibility. Handshape and movement are the parameters most often misperceived; location is the most robust.

### Cited Findings
- **ASL point-light displays.** Poizner, Bellugi & Lutes-Driscoll (1981), *J. Exp. Psychol.: Human Perception and Performance* 7, 430–440. Deaf signers matched lexical signs shown as dynamic point-light displays (head, shoulder, arm and finger markers) to normal video with high accuracy. They also identified constant-handshape signs and morphological inflections. When points were removed, **only fingertip motion was necessary for identification**; no other pair of points was. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract content via a citation index and citing papers; no accuracy percentage obtained). — [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/516cdb43d15a38c0a62ebf7b02c08d95); [Springer 10.3758/BF03202858, citing paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03202858)
- **ASL with 27 moving spots.** Tartter & Knowlton (1981), "Perception of sign language from an array of 27 moving spots", *Nature* 289, 676–678, doi:10.1038/289676a0. Fluent ASL users "communicate easily" seeing only 27 light spots placed on the hands and face. The paper includes a conversation transcript. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via index snippet). — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/289676a0.pdf); [Scilit](https://scilit.net/publications/8d00e37ebe7b6555a17c5141b6fb988e)
- **ASL at very low information rates.** Sperling, Landy, Cohen & Pavel (1985), *CVGIP* 31, 335–391. Seventeen image transformations (binary or cartoon, reduced resolution) were tested with 38 Deaf participants on 87 ASL sequences of 1–2 s each. Some transformations allow telephone-rate ASL at 4,800 bit/s, and high intelligibility was reached at 9,600 bit/s. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via search snippets; the OCR intelligibility figure was garbled). — [CVGIP85 PDF](https://dada.cs.washington.edu/research/mobileasl/downloads/CVGIP85.pdf)
- **Normalised intelligibility of 0.86.** Parish, Sperling & Landy (1990) report that one condition of the 1985 study reached normalised intelligibility of **0.86** relative to full-bandwidth sequences. They attribute this to the large spatial redundancy of the ASL signal. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY. — [Parish et al. 1990 PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~whipl/staff/sperling/PDFs/Parish+Sperling_ASL_JEP_1990.pdf)
- **ASL temporal subsampling.** Parish, Sperling & Landy (1990), *JEP:HPP* 16(2), 282–294. Thirty-two experienced ASL signers viewed isolated signs. Choosing frames at event boundaries (local minima of frame-to-frame change) gave significantly higher intelligibility than taking every m-th frame. The advantage was larger for static-frame displays and smaller for binary images. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via PubMed snippet). — [PubMed 2142199](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2142199)
- **ASL, additive noise.** Pavel, Sperling, Riedl & Vanderbeek (1987), *JOSA A* 4(12), 2355ff. This study measured isolated-sign intelligibility as a function of signal-to-noise ratio. I did not obtain the results. Label: EXISTS. — [PDF](https://sites.socsci.uci.edu/~whipl/staff/sperling/PDFs/Pavel_et_al_ASL_JOSA_1987.pdf)
- **ASL mobile video, frame rate and bit rate.** Cavender, Ladner & Riskin (2006, ASSETS) tested 10 versus 15 fps, 15/20/25 kbit/s, and region-of-interest settings of 0/−6/−12 (higher face quality). Deaf respondents *preferred* **10 fps** at a fixed bit rate, because each frame was of better quality. They preferred a moderate face region of interest (−6) and higher bit rates. These were ratings, not comprehension scores. Label: VERIFIED (as described in the full text of Tran et al. CHI 2013 extended abstract, which I read). — [Tran et al. CHI13](https://mobileasl.cs.washington.edu/downloads/CHI13-tran.pdf)
- **ASL intelligibility ceiling.** Tran, Riskin, Ladner & Wobbrock (2015), *ACM TACCESS*, "Evaluating intelligibility and battery drain of mobile sign language video transmitted at low frame rates and bit rates". There was an "intelligibility ceiling effect": frame rates **above 10 fps did not improve perceived intelligibility**, and bit rates above 60 kbit/s gave diminishing returns. The web study crossed 1/5/10/15 fps with 15/30/60/120 kbit/s. A companion ASSETS 2014 lab study found intelligible conversation **at 10 fps and 50 kbit/s**. The ITU-T recommendation is 25 fps at 100 kbit/s or more. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via index; design VERIFIED from the CHI 2013 text). — [Stevens portal](https://researchwith.stevens.edu/en/publications/evaluating-intelligibility-and-battery-drain-of-mobile-sign-langu/); [CHI13](https://mobileasl.cs.washington.edu/downloads/CHI13-tran.pdf)
- **ASL learning from very low frame rates.** Johnson & Caird found **1 and 5 fps sufficient** for novices to learn ten single-sign ASL videos. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (cited by Cherniavsky et al., ASSETS 2009; hearing novices, not Deaf signers). — [Cherniavsky et al.](https://dada.cs.washington.edu/research/mobileasl/downloads/assets090-cherniavsky.pdf)
- **BSL misperception by parameter.** Orfanidou, Adam, McQueen & Morgan (2009), *Memory & Cognition* 37(3), 302–315, doi:10.3758/MC.37.3.302. Deaf BSL signers from three age-of-acquisition groups did sign-spotting in nonsense contexts. Their misperception errors changed **movement and handshape** more than **location**, with differences by age of acquisition. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via index snippets). — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/MC.37.3.302)

### Inferences
- Viewers do not need dense surface or appearance detail. Coarse skeletal kinematics with fingertip trajectories are enough, which strongly supports skeleton-only representations. Fingertips carry the identity (Poizner et al.), so augmentation must preserve fingertip paths and inter-fingertip relationships more strictly than wrist-to-shoulder geometry.
- A ceiling at about 10 fps, plus better intelligibility when frames are sampled at event boundaries, implies that **temporal resampling or frame dropping to about 10 fps or more is perceptually safe** when holds and boundaries are kept. Uniform sampling below about 5 fps is not well supported for Deaf viewers.
- Location is the most robustly perceived parameter (Orfanidou et al.) and is perceived non-categorically (see Section 6). Small location jitter is unlikely to change perceived identity, except for near-minimal pairs (see `linguistics.md` §1.5 and §2.6). Handshape and movement errors are the ones viewers actually make. Augmentation of handshape and movement should therefore be the more conservative.

### Gaps
- I did not obtain recognition accuracy percentages for the point-light studies, or the number of lights at which performance falls off.
- I did not obtain the Pavel et al. (1987) signal-to-noise thresholds or the Sperling et al. (1985) per-condition resolution and intelligibility tables. The scanned PDFs could not be extracted.
- I found no controlled human study of missing or occluded hands, for example one hand removed, on isolated-sign recognition.

## 5. Signing-avatar intelligibility: which motion-parameter changes hurt?

### Takeaway
Avatar studies consistently show that **timing structure** (pauses and transitions), **mouthing and facial channels** and naturalness, meaning motion-capture-driven over procedural animation, drive comprehension and acceptance. Deaf viewers do not require human-identical timing. I found **no avatar study that reports numeric thresholds for amplitude, location offset or handshape precision**.

### Cited Findings
- **DGS.** Kipp, Nguyen, Heloir & Matthes (2011), "Assessing the deaf user perspective on sign language avatars", ASSETS 2011. Two focus groups (N=8) and an accessible online study (N=330). Mouthing was flagged as particularly important for DGS comprehension; its absence or poor synchronisation was highly disturbing. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via DFKI and ASSETS pages; the mouthing detail is from a later review). — [ASSETS 2011](https://assets11.sigaccess.org/29.html); [DFKI](https://dfki.de/web/forschung/projekte-publikationen/publikation/5468)
- **DGS.** Kipp, Heloir & Nguyen (2011), "Sign language avatars: animation and comprehensibility", IVA 2011. This introduced "delta testing", which measures avatar comprehensibility relative to a human-signer video baseline. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY. — [DFKI](https://www.dfki.de/web/forschung/projekte-publikationen/publikation/5469)
- **ASL.** Al-Khazraji et al. (2021). Signers did not want human-identical timing: they preferred faster signs, slower transitions and human-like pauses (see Section 3). Label: VERIFIED (abstract). — [NJIT](https://researchwith.njit.edu/en/publications/at-a-different-pace-evaluating-whether-users-prefer-timing-parame/)
- **ASL.** Huenerfauth (2008, 2009). Linguistically motivated pause insertion and timing significantly improved comprehension and Likert understandability ratings (see Section 3). Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY. — [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F1530064.1530067)
- **LSF.** Naert, Larboulette & Gibet (2020), LSF-ANIMAL corpus, LREC 2020. Motion-capture-driven avatar signs were rated for intelligibility, accuracy and realism by 41 participants, including native LSF signers. The authors note that procedurally animated (robotic) avatars are rejected by the community, which motivates editing captured motion rather than synthesising it from scratch. Label: VERIFIED-SECONDARY (abstract via index snippets; rating numbers not obtained). — [HAL](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-03005767); [ACL Anthology](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2020.lrec-1.736)

### Inferences
- Moderate global speed scaling is unlikely to hurt human comprehension; Deaf users even like faster signs. What matters more is keeping the hold, pause and transition structure intact. For augmentation, warping the time inside a sign is safer than changing the hold-to-movement proportions.
- Because avatar studies report naturalness penalties for robotic motion, augmentations that produce jerky or physically implausible motion are the realistic risk. Examples are unsmoothed per-frame jitter, joint-limit violations and body interpenetration. This supports the project's geometry-aware constraint stage.

### Gaps
- I found no avatar study that systematically varied amplitude (signing-space scale), location offset in centimetres, or handshape joint-angle error and reported a threshold. The Kacorri and Gibet work found addresses facial expression and corpus quality, not these parameters. Their full texts were not read.
- The SGNify (CVPR 2023) perceptual study, in which signers recognised reconstructed avatars, could not be fetched (PDF over 10 MB).

## 6. Categorical-perception continua: step sizes and boundaries

### Takeaway
Across ASL and LSF, contrastive handshape continua show categorical perception in Deaf signers. Place-of-articulation continua do not. The continua are built as **11-step** morphs (avatar or computer-generated) between two endpoint handshapes or locations. I could not extract the physical step sizes (joint angles or centimetres) or the boundary step numbers from any accessible text. Replications are mixed for handshape.

### Cited Findings
- **LSF.** GLOW 45 (2022) abstract 106, "Categorical Perception in French Sign Language (LSF)". The authors are not named in the extracted text; a later DGfS 2026 abstract by Navarrete-González et al. may be from the same group. Four pairs of static pseudo-signs were made into **11-step continua with an avatar (Poser Pro)**. For both handshape and place of articulation there was one phonemic continuum and one allophonic continuum. Participants were 21 Deaf early signers (LSF before age 6) and 24 hearing non-signers, tested online. In signers' accuracy, there was a categorical-perception effect for the **phonemic handshape pair only**: none for the allophonic handshape pair and **none for place of articulation**. Reaction times showed the opposite, puzzling pattern, which the authors caveat because testing was online. The design "largely follows" Emmorey et al. (2003). ### Inferences
- Within-category handshape perturbations (inside the allophonic range) do not produce categorical-perception boundaries. Perturbation across a phonemic contrast does, somewhere in the middle of an 11-step morph. Without physical step sizes, a practical inference is that handshape jitter should stay **well under half the endpoint-to-endpoint distance** of the nearest contrasting handshape. Ideally it should be tied to measured natural within-signer variability rather than a perceptual threshold.
- The absence of categorical perception for place of articulation is consistent with Section 4: location is perceived gradiently and robustly. However, gradient perception does not mean there is no limit, because location minimal pairs exist.
